1815–1852 · mathematician

Ada Lovelace

Translator and annotator of the Analytical Engine. In 1843 she published seven notes, signed A.A.L., on a machine that was never built — and used them to argue that an engine directed by punched cards could operate on symbols, and not on numbers alone.

Projects

Note G — the Bernoulli programme

Scientific Memoirs, volume III · 1843

A table of operations for computing the Bernoulli numbers on the Analytical Engine, set out as columns of variables and the ordered operations that transform them, with the working cycles marked so that a group of operations can be repeated. It is generally read as the first published algorithm written for a machine to carry out. It is also the note in which she draws the boundary herself: the engine “has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform.”

The translation of Menabrea's Sketch

From the French of L. F. Menabrea · 1842–1843

Luigi Menabrea, who had heard Babbage lecture in Turin in 1840, published his account of the Analytical Engine in French in 1842. She translated it into English and then appended notes A to G, which together run to roughly three times the length of the paper they annotate — a commentary that became the primary description of the machine in English. The work is signed A.A.L. rather than with her name.

Note A — the loom and the general machine

On what separates the two engines · 1843

The note that divides the Difference Engine, which computes and tabulates by one fixed method, from the Analytical Engine, which is directed by cards and can be given a different problem without being rebuilt: “the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves.” From there she reasons past arithmetic altogether — anything whose relations can be written as symbols, the pitches of a harmony among them, could in principle be composed by the same machinery.
